Personal QA tooling

Turning scattered context into reviewable QA work

A personal QA workspace with version control and strong opinions about how AI should behave. I designed the workflow to bring ticket context, prior findings, and release information into runbooks I could actually work from.

Problem

A ticket rarely arrives with everything needed to test it. The useful context might be in a code change, an old finding, a discussion, or a release note. I kept having to reconstruct the task before I could investigate it.

I wanted that groundwork in one usable packet: what should happen, what had gone wrong before, where to look for regressions, and which sources the system could not check. Missing context needed a label, not a confident guess.

Decisions and implementation

I defined the behavior, configured the tools, and directed AI-assisted implementation. Then I tried the results on real tasks. Runbooks, investigation tools, evidence-review views, and shared-artifact practices became parts of the same workflow, each with a job to earn.

The central rule was simple: say when a source could not be checked. An unavailable system and an empty result mean different things. Preparation, investigation, independent review, and the human decision also stayed separate, so a finished packet could not quietly become a finished test.

For shared outputs, I used review before external action, one writer for the current artifact, and recovery steps when work stopped partway through. These were practices to test and revise as the tools changed.

The Kanban board got a rethink too. Ownership lanes looked organized but did a poor job of showing progress, so I changed them to pipeline stages. Stale, missing, disconnected, and empty data each needed their own state. I also removed convincing sample cards from the live starting view. Fictional work should not look ready to pick up.

Validation and refinement

One review caught a small-looking change with a large consequence: shortening a comment had made its claims stronger than the investigation notes. I changed the review step to compare each claim with its source before the comment went out. Brevity had to keep the facts attached.

A repaired pilot passing its original sample was a good start; I asked for more random trials. An impact viewer went unused, so its information moved into a surface I already used. A parallel-work pipeline was retired when its overhead outweighed its help. Sometimes the useful improvement was one less thing to maintain.

Evidence and output

The workflow produced ticket-specific runbooks and review views. Its historical release-reporting tools brought Jira status, GitHub pull requests, and release discussions into scope and merge updates distributed through Google Docs and Confluence. Colleagues used those documents to follow the release.

I wrote the QA acceptance verdicts. The surrounding prose was generated or aggregated through the AI-assisted workflow. The shared output was the document; the tools remained personally operated.

Limits and lessons

These were tools I operated for my QA work. The release-documentation automation was later retired, and its history includes stale information and environment distinctions that needed correction. Useful output still needed upkeep.

The test I keep coming back to: can I start work from this packet, and can I see where its knowledge runs out? Retrieval, review, and honest gaps all have to work together.
