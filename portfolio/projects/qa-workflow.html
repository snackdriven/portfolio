Personal QA tooling

Turning scattered context into reviewable QA work

I shaped a personal workflow system that brings ticket context, prior findings, and release information into the preparation and review of QA work.

Problem

QA work often starts before the first test. The relevant context can sit across a ticket, a code change, a discussion, a previous finding, and release information. Reassembling it for each task takes attention away from the investigation.

I wanted a preparation packet I could work from: behavior under test, relevant history, likely regression areas, and an honest account of anything the system could not retrieve.

Decisions and implementation

I defined the workflow behavior, configured the tools, directed AI-assisted implementation, and refined the results against real tasks. Related pieces included preparation and investigation tools, ticket-specific runbooks, evidence-review views, and practices for shared artifacts. They support one personal workflow rather than separate headline products.

The central rule was that an unavailable source must remain visibly unavailable. It cannot become an empty result that implies nothing relevant exists. I also kept preparation, investigation, independent review, and a human decision as separate steps.

For shared outputs, I used review before external action, one writer for the current artifact, and recovery steps when work stopped partway through. These were practices to test and revise as the tools changed.

Validation and refinement

Real use exposed gaps. In one review, shortened comment text overstated what the investigation notes supported. I changed the review step to compare each claim against the underlying notes before the comment went out. A polished summary only helps when its claims still match the evidence.

I also asked for additional random trials after a repaired pilot passed its initial sample. When a separate impact viewer went unused, I moved its information into an existing work surface. A parallel-work pipeline was retired when its overhead did not earn a place in the workflow.

Evidence and output

The system produced ticket-specific runbooks and review views. A historical release-documentation workflow assembled scope, status, and merge information into stakeholder documents distributed through Google Docs and Confluence. Colleagues used those documents.

My QA acceptance verdicts in those documents were user-authored. The surrounding prose was generated or aggregated through the AI-assisted workflow. Use of the documents does not imply coworkers installed or adopted the underlying tools.

Limits and lessons

This was personal tooling, not organization-wide infrastructure. The release-documentation automation was later retired. Its useful output also revealed maintenance issues, including stale information and environment distinctions that needed correction.

The practical test for a packet is whether I can begin work from it and see what it has not checked. That is why the review and missing-input states matter as much as the retrieval itself.
